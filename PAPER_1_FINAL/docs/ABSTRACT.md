# Abstract

**Do Pre-Harvest Weather Shocks Affect Agricultural Prices? Evidence from Bangladesh**

Growing-season weather in South Asia is becoming more erratic, and price spikes in onion and potato are a recurring food-security
concern in Bangladesh. Whether local weather shocks during the growing season move local market prices is an open empirical question.
We ask whether pre-harvest weather shocks causally affect wholesale onion and potato prices. We assemble 34,341
market-commodity-month wholesale prices from the Department of Agricultural Marketing (220 markets, 64 districts, 2000-2026) and
merge them with district-level ERA5-Land temperature and ERA5 and CHIRPS precipitation. Exposure is the number of months in the
crop-specific pre-harvest window (October-March for onion, October-February for potato) with temperature or rainfall more than 1.5
standard deviations from 1991-2020 normals, assigned to the following marketing year. Market-by-commodity and commodity-by-month fixed
effects isolate within-market deviations from national price movements; standard errors are clustered by district. We find a
precisely estimated zero: one additional shocked month changes wholesale prices by -0.14% (95% CI -0.47% to
+0.20%). Next season's shocks have no effect (p = 0.76), onion and potato do not
differ, and the response does not rise with distance from Dhaka. Heat months carry a positive coefficient (+0.85%),
but a larger one appears for imported onion and it is threshold-sensitive, so we do not interpret it causally. Retail prices follow
wholesale prices with an elasticity of 0.70. Local weather shocks appear to be arbitraged away across integrated markets;
price-risk policy should target national supply shocks - import timing, stocks and national early warning - rather than district weather.
